# Opportunity Discovery Framework

**Status:** operating proposal  
**Purpose:** compare opportunities without falling in love with an idea too early.

## 1. What counts as an opportunity

An opportunity is not simply a large market, an annoying app, or a feature competitors lack.

A credible opportunity combines:

- a clearly identifiable customer and use case;
- a recurring or high-consequence problem;
- an inadequate existing workaround;
- a plausible route to reach customers;
- evidence that someone will change behaviour or pay;
- delivery economics that can work for a small founder-led business.

## 2. Opportunity brief

Create one brief per candidate. Keep it to one or two pages initially.

Required fields:

- **Working name**
- **Customer segment:** who experiences the problem?
- **Job to be done:** what are they trying to accomplish?
- **Trigger:** when does the problem occur?
- **Current workflow:** what do they use today?
- **Pain:** time, money, risk, lost revenue, uncertainty, frustration
- **Frequency and severity**
- **Alternatives and competitors**
- **Why current alternatives fail:** specific evidence, not assumptions
- **Buyer and user:** same person or different?
- **Potential business model**
- **Reachability:** how can the first users be reached within current constraints?
- **Operational burden:** what must the business do manually?
- **Regulatory / trust / safety concerns**
- **Cheapest falsifiable test**
- **Evidence and confidence**
- **Reasons to stop**

## 3. Evidence ladder

Never collapse these states into one vague status such as "validated".

1. **Hypothesis** — plausible idea; no external evidence.
2. **Desk research** — public information about the market and alternatives.
3. **Access path** — a possible introduction or channel; not a conversation.
4. **Problem conversation** — a person describes their own recent experience.
5. **Repeated pain** — independent people describe materially similar problems.
6. **Behavioural evidence** — a person takes a meaningful action: shares workflow data voluntarily, requests a solution, tries a manual workaround, or introduces a buyer.
7. **Commercial intent** — asks about price, procurement, or a pilot with a defined scope.
8. **Payment / paid use** — money changes hands for the solution.

A higher state requires evidence for that state. Website visits, likes, survey enthusiasm, and a friendly promise are not substitutes for usage or payment.

## 4. Evaluation rubric

Score each dimension from 1 (weak / unknown) to 5 (strong evidence). Add a confidence label: low, medium, or high. Unknowns must not receive a high score.

| Dimension | What a high score means |
|---|---|
| Pain intensity | Problem creates meaningful time, money, risk, or lost opportunity |
| Frequency | Happens often enough to support recurring use or repeat transactions |
| Existing alternatives | Current solutions leave a demonstrable gap |
| Willingness to pay | Buyer has paid, budgeted, or shown concrete commercial intent |
| Reachability | First users can be reached through a realistic, affordable channel |
| Founder fit | Can be tested and delivered within founder skills and constraints |
| Time to evidence | A cheap experiment can produce meaningful evidence quickly |
| Unit economics | Plausible gross margin after acquisition, service, refunds, and support |
| Expansion potential | Adjacent segments share the same underlying problem and workflow |
| Risk / complexity | Legal, trust, data, safety, and operational risks are manageable |

Do not reduce the decision to a single total score. A fatal weakness in reachability, willingness to pay, unit economics, or regulatory feasibility cannot be hidden by a large market score.

## 5. First candidate set

These are research candidates, not ranked opportunities:

- **Tour operations:** operator workflow, booking coordination, resource availability, and distribution.
- **Rental housing:** trustworthy, current listings; duplicate detection; availability confirmation; viewing coordination; transparent total cost.
- **Travel/activity discovery:** comparing fragmented offers and completing reliable bookings across local suppliers.

A third candidate may be added only when a concrete problem and customer segment can be named. Avoid generating endless idea lists.

## 6. Research sequence

For each candidate:

1. Map customer segments and the end-to-end job.
2. Identify incumbent products, informal channels, and manual workarounds.
3. Find where the current workflow breaks and who bears the cost.
4. Check how the market makes money today.
5. Identify a reachable first user group without violating founder constraints.
6. Design the smallest test that could disprove the hypothesis.
7. Review evidence and decide: continue, narrow, pivot, or stop.

No arbitrary interview quotas or deadlines. If access is unavailable, record the blocker and choose a different low-cost evidence source. Do not call an inconclusive test a failure of the market.

## 7. Decision outcomes

- **Continue discovery:** important unknowns remain and a credible next test exists.
- **Narrow:** pain is real but only in a smaller segment or workflow.
- **Prototype:** repeated pain and a specific evaluation opportunity justify a reversible build.
- **Commercial pilot:** named buyer, scope, consent, safeguards, and commercial path exist.
- **Pivot:** evidence points to a different customer, problem, or workflow.
- **Stop:** no credible pain, differentiation, reachability, or viable economics.

Every decision should record the evidence, uncertainty, and next review condition.
